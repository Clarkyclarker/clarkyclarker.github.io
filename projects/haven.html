Haven: Robotic Tool Vending Machine

Based on Haven Final Report: Executive Summary, Sections 4-5, and Appendix S

Overview

Haven is a secure, automated tool checkout kiosk designed for student-run makerspaces. The system was built for the UBC Integrated Engineering shop, where over 250 users and 30+ capstone projects create heavy demand for shared tools. The final concept combines a rotary storage system, a vertical Cartesian XY gantry, RFID-based tool tracking, and a compact enclosure into one self-serve kiosk.

My work focused on the mechanical and mechatronics side: turning the storage concept into manufacturable hardware, designing and assembling the gantry, supporting the rotary tool cake and enclosure design, and validating the integrated dispense and return workflow through testing.

Mechanical Design

The mechanical system was split into two main moving subassemblies: the gantry delivery system and the rotary tool cake. The gantry handles vertical and horizontal tool transfer, while the cake stores tools in indexed rotary layers. This separation made the system modular, easier to prototype, and easier to debug during integration.

Gantry Subassembly

The gantry acts as the delivery mechanism. It moves a transfer slice between storage levels and the pickup window, allowing Haven to retrieve or return tools without exposing the rest of the inventory.

Cake Subassembly

The cake acts as the storage mechanism. Each layer stores multiple tools around a circular track, while rotating inner walls push tools between storage positions and the gantry transfer opening.

Gantry Subassembly

The gantry was designed as a vertically mounted Cartesian XY system. CoreXY and cantilever architectures were considered, but the final design used lead screws and linear rails because the vertical axis needed to resist gravity loads reliably. A belt-driven vertical system would have depended on belt tension and motor holding torque, increasing the risk of drift, heating, and slippage under load.

  • Used dual vertical lead screws and one horizontal lead screw to move the transfer slice between storage layers and the user bay.
  • Used linear rails to guide the carriage and prevent unwanted rotation or racking.
  • Selected MGN9H rails based on load capacity, cost, and a worst-case moving mass calculation with a safety factor.
  • Selected 8 mm lead screws to reach the required dispense speed while staying compatible with stepper motor performance.
  • Relocated motors and refined carriage plates after early prototypes revealed mounting, alignment, and axial-support issues.

Report reference: Section 4.1.1 Tool Cake, Section 4.1.2 Drivetrain, Appendix D-F

Cake Subassembly

The rotary tool cake was the storage mechanism. Instead of using drawers or lockers, Haven stores tools around circular layers. The open sector of each layer acts as a transfer interface; when the gantry reaches the correct level, the cake indexes to present the requested tool to the transfer slice.

  • Sprocket: 3D-printed ABS hub that interfaces with the drivetrain and rotates the inner walls.
  • Inner walls: ABS wall elements that push tools around the cake during indexing.
  • Outer walls: Modular 3D-printed PLA walls that help retain tools on the plate.
  • Cake plate: Waterjet ABS plate bonded to a 3D-printed rib structure for stiffness and manufacturability.
  • Drivetrain: NEMA 17 stepper motor, 10:1 planetary gearbox, timing belt, pulleys, and tensioning mount for quiet indexed rotation.

Mechanical Testing

Testing followed the same split as the design: first validating the gantry and cake as individual subassemblies, then combining them during integrated dispense and return cycles. This made it easier to isolate mechanical issues before full system testing.

Gantry Testing

The gantry was first tested as a single vertical leg, then as a full XY mechanism, and finally inside the enclosure. It supported 2.5 kg during early static/dynamic tests and later moved a 3.5 kg load during integrated dynamic testing without step skipping or overheating. The final gantry achieved the required storage-level coverage and reached speeds up to 80 mm/s.

Cake Testing

The cake plate was checked with FEA for a 5 kg distributed load and a 1 kg edge load, with maximum deflections of 0.16 mm and 0.18 mm. Physical drivetrain tests validated 60-degree indexed rotation. When tool friction caused the cake plate to rotate unintentionally, a set screw and reduced-contact geometry were added to improve positional consistency.

  • Integrated testing completed 152 dispense and return cycles using tools with different geometries across multiple storage levels.
  • Most mechanical failures were concentrated in the return workflow, especially for longer and heavier tools, showing that the transfer interface was the key area for refinement.
  • Design refinements included stiffening the transfer slice, constraining the cake plate, quieting motor motion through driver configuration, and improving local clearances at the cake-slice interface.

Results & Mechatronics Takeaways

  • Delivered a working end-to-end prototype that could authenticate users, retrieve tools from storage, dispense them to a user bay, and support returns.
  • Met the 30-tool capacity target using six rotary layers with five tools per layer.
  • Met the 30-second dispense-cycle target from website request to user bay.
  • Achieved over 96% success for moderate-size tools and approximately 93% reliability overall during integrated testing.
  • Key mechatronics lesson: reliable automation depended less on any single subsystem and more on the physical interface between the gantry, rotary cake, sensors, and control logic.
